RE:           IntraFi's Insured Cash Sweep (ICS) Program and the Rule 1105(c) Administrator Bond

You asked me to research IntraFi's Insured Cash Sweep (ICS) program and compare its protections with those of the fund administrator bond required under Rule 1105(c), in light of Epiq's recent proposals. My analysis follows.

In short, ICS and the bond protect against different risks. ICS insures deposits against the failure of the banks holding them, while the Rule 1105(c) bond is conditioned on the administrator's faithful performance of its duties. ICS therefore cannot replace the bond, though it could supplement it. Epiq's second proposal, sizing the bond to the balance still at risk, fits within the rule.

Epiq proposes two alternatives to bonding the full value of settlement funds: placing funds through ICS, and calculating bond coverage on the balance remaining in the fund at each purchase or renewal. Epiq's stated premise is that the bond protects against bank failure. Epiq also suggests holding funds in short-term Treasury securities.

How ICS Works

ICS spreads one large deposit across many FDIC-insured banks in amounts under the $250,000 limit, while the depositor works directly with a single relationship bank and receives one statement.[1] The relationship bank acts as custodian for the placed deposits, with BNY Mellon as subcustodian.[2]

Coverage depends on pass-through insurance. The FDIC recognizes a fiduciary relationship only if it is expressly disclosed in the bank's deposit account records; insurance then passes to the principal.[3] If the records fall short, the deposit is insured only to the nominal depositor, capped at $250,000.[4]

Five practical limits matter for a Fair Fund:

1.     Capacity is set by the offering bank, not by one program cap. Published limits run from $100 million to $265 million per depositor.[5] Epiq's recollection of "several hundred million" sits at or above the top of that range.

2.     Offering banks may charge no enrollment fee, and the rate paid varies by bank.[6] The cost of ICS is therefore likely to show up as yield rather than a fee, though actual terms depend on the bank's agreement.

3.     Balances at the placing bank can exceed the insured limit before deposits settle and after withdrawals settle.[7] Distribution checks and wires clear through that bank, so some uninsured exposure remains during payment.

4.     The depositor must disclose other banks where it holds funds so those banks are excluded; otherwise balances aggregate and can exceed the limit.[8]

5.     The FDIC aims to pay insured deposits within two business days, but deposits placed through a fiduciary can take longer because the FDIC needs supplemental records.[9]

What the Rule 1105(c) Bond Covers

Rule 1105(c) requires an administrator who is not a Commission employee to obtain a bond "in the manner prescribed in 11 U.S.C. 322," in an amount the Commission approves. The cost may be paid as a cost of administration, and the Commission may waive the bond for good cause shown.[10] Section 322 requires a bond in favor of the United States conditioned on faithful performance of official duties, and any claim on the bond must be brought within two years of discharge.[11]

The bond therefore answers for the administrator's conduct: misdirected payments, misappropriation, or other failures in how it handles the fund. Nothing in the rule or the statute suggests it answers for a loss caused solely by the depository bank's failure. The bond form itself would control, and I have not reviewed one.

Two other provisions of Rule 1105 bear on cost. Administration expenses are paid first from interest earned on the fund and then from the corpus.[12] The bond is cancelled only after the Commission approves the final accounting.[13] When interest falls short, premiums come out of money that would otherwise reach investors, and they continue until the fund closes.

Epiq's Proposals

ICS in place of the bond. Because the bond secures administrator performance rather than deposit safety, ICS would leave that risk uncovered and would not satisfy Rule 1105(c) without a good-cause waiver. It could still serve as an added layer for balances that would otherwise sit uninsured at the escrow bank, provided staff confirm the account titling meets pass-through requirements and the sweep timing works with positive pay and check clearing.

Bond only funds on deposit. This proposal fits the current rule. Rule 1105(c) leaves the bond amount to the Commission, and the Enforcement Director holds delegated authority to set or waive it for administrators the Director appoints from the Commission's approved pool in administrative proceedings.[14] Resetting the amount at each renewal to the balance still at risk would cut premiums on money already distributed without reducing protection for funds the administrator still controls. Any reduction should keep the original limit for claims arising from acts before the reset, so coverage for earlier distributions is not lost.

Treasury securities. Treasury investments protect principal while funds await distribution, but they do not reach the period after funds move to the escrow bank for payment, which is where depository risk concentrates. Whether a given fund is already held at Treasury should be confirmed from that fund's records.

Recommendation

I recommend declining Epiq's first proposal as a substitute for the bond and pursuing its second, with the safeguards noted above.
