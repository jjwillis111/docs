Slide 1: Where Does the Money Go?

Target time: 1:50 (running total 1:50)

I want to start with a fairly simple question. When the SEC goes after someone for securities fraud, and a court orders that person to give up the money they made, where does that money actually go? I think most of us would assume it goes back to the investors. After all, if the whole point is to protect investors, that seems like the obvious answer.

But the law is considerably less settled than you might expect. And, somewhat surprisingly, the Supreme Court has now addressed this remedy three times since 2017 without settling what kind of remedy it is, or where the money has to go. My paper looks at what happened after the Court’s decision in Sripetch earlier this year, and why a debate that sounds pretty technical, whether disgorgement is legal or equitable, could ultimately shape what happens to billions of dollars.

The argument I’ll come back to is pretty straightforward: whatever label the Court eventually chooses, paying victims first appears easiest to defend.

First, a little background. Disgorgement is a remedy designed to take away the profits someone obtained through wrongdoing. Think of a person who manipulates a stock and makes a million dollars. The SEC might ask a court to order that person to surrender the million dollars.

That’s different from measuring what each individual investor lost. Courts have long measured this kind of relief by the defendant’s gain rather than the plaintiff’s loss.

That difference raises a question about what the SEC has to prove. Can it recover a wrongdoer’s profits even if the investors it’s trying to protect can’t show a measurable financial loss? Sripetch answered that one.

What it didn’t answer is whether the remedy is legal or equitable, or where the money has to go once the SEC recovers it. Those are the two open questions my paper is about. To see why they are still open, we need to go back a few years.

Slide 2: Three Decisions and One Statute Change

Target time: 1:40 (running total 3:30).

In 2017, the Supreme Court decided Kokesh. The question there was whether disgorgement counted as a penalty for purposes of a federal statute of limitations. The Court said yes. It emphasized that disgorgement enforced public laws, could serve punitive purposes, and didn’t always compensate victims. Sometimes the money went to the Treasury instead. But Kokesh expressly didn’t decide whether courts had authority to order disgorgement in the first place.

Three years later came Liu. There, the Court held that disgorgement could qualify as equitable relief under the Exchange Act, but with important limits. It generally couldn’t exceed the wrongdoer’s net profits, and it had to be awarded for victims. The Court still left a practical question unanswered: what if returning the money to investors simply isn’t feasible?

Then, in 2026, Sripetch held that the SEC doesn’t have to prove that investors suffered pecuniary, or financial, loss. The Court recognized that traditional equitable remedies can focus on a wrongdoer’s gains even when a victim can’t show a measurable loss. So those three decisions each resolved something important. But none settled exactly what the SEC may do with money it can’t distribute.

Between Liu and Sripetch, Congress added another wrinkle. Six months after Liu, Congress amended the Exchange Act. The old provision, section 78u(d)(5), authorizes equitable relief that may be appropriate or necessary for the benefit of investors. The new provision, subsection (d)(7), expressly authorizes courts to order disgorgement. But it doesn’t use the words equitable relief, and it doesn’t say for the benefit of investors.

That difference in wording is one reason the remedy’s status is in doubt. Did Congress simply make explicit the equitable remedy the Court had already recognized in Liu? Or did Congress create a separate legal remedy with different rules?

Slide 3: Question 1: Legal or Equitable?

Target time: 0:54 (running total 4:25).

The federal courts of appeals have already divided on that question. The Fifth Circuit, in Hallam, read the amended statute as authorizing disgorgement in a legal sense, distinct from the equitable disgorgement recognized in Liu, without deciding whether equitable disgorgement survived the amendments. The Second Circuit, in Ahmed, read the term as equitable disgorgement as recognized in Liu, still subject to traditional equitable limits.

In Sripetch, the majority declined to resolve the question. It simply assumed, without deciding, that disgorgement under subsection (d)(7) remains equitable. That didn’t change the result, because the Court ruled for the SEC even assuming the victims requirement applies.

Justice Thomas wrote separately. He pointed out that Congress separated disgorgement from equitable remedies, and he would hold, in a future case, that disgorgement is now a legal remedy. He reasoned that if disgorgement were simply equitable relief, the new paragraph would be su-per-flu-ous. He also said the Court will soon need to address the question.

Slide 4: Why the Label Matters

Target time: 1:10 (running total 5:34).

So why should anyone outside a securities class care whether we call this legal or equitable? Because equity traditionally comes with limits. Under Liu, a properly limited equitable disgorgement award is supposed to be for victims. If the remedy is instead legal, those equitable limits might not automatically apply.

But that raises a different problem. If the remedy is legal, the Seventh Amendment gives the defendant a right to a jury. In Jark-e-zy, the Supreme Court held that defendants are entitled to a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties. Justice Thomas’s concurrence in Sripetch goes further. He would recognize disgorgement as a legal remedy for which the Seventh Amendment requires a jury trial. The majority referred to the right to a jury only in discussing penalties, citing Jarkesy.

There’s also a related penalty problem. If the government collects money that isn’t tied to victims, that money could share the features that led Kokesh to call disgorgement a penalty.

So the question cuts in more than one direction. If (d)(7) is a separate legal remedy, Treasury deposits could become possible in principle, and the Eleventh Circuit reached a similar result in Spartan on statutory grounds. But defendants could claim a right to a jury.

Slide 5: Question 2: What if Payment Is Infeasible?

Target time: 0:56 (running total 6:30).

That brings us to the second major question in my paper. What happens when distribution to investors is infeasible? Liu expressly reserved whether disgorgement remains available in that situation, and Sripetch said the question was still open.

There are two ways to think about it. One is that an equitable award must actually benefit investors, so money paid to no investor can’t satisfy the rule. The other is that disgorgement still serves a legitimate purpose by preventing wrongdoers from keeping illicit profits, even if the Treasury is the only realistic destination.

The Eleventh Circuit adopted a position favorable to the SEC in Spartan Securities Group. It held that the amended statute permits Treasury-bound disgorgement without requiring investor benefit. Alternatively, it said equity allowed that result where the parties had stipulated that distribution was infeasible. That alternative holding is narrower, because it depends on a record of infeasibility. It’s the answer of one court of appeals, and the Supreme Court hasn’t answered the question.

Slide 6: Spartan Is the Case to Watch

Target time: 0:55 (running total 7:25).

Spartan is especially timely for this presentation. The defendants petitioned the Supreme Court for review, and one of their questions asks whether, after Liu and Sripetch, courts may order disgorgement to the Treasury where no investor was harmed. The Court asked the government to respond, and that response was originally due November 23. On November 20, the deadline was extended to December 23. My draft still says November 23, so I’ll update that before the final version is due.

I want to be careful about what that means. The Court requesting a response isn’t the same thing as agreeing to hear the case. And the petition’s other question, concerning Rule 10b-5, is outside my paper, so a grant limited to that question would leave disgorgement untouched.

Still, a grant on the disgorgement question would put the Eleventh Circuit’s holding on Treasury-bound disgorgement before the Court. A denial would leave the Eleventh Circuit’s rule in place there and the question open elsewhere.

Slide 7: The Numbers Need Context

Target time: 1:33 (running total 8:58)..

Now, I want to spend a minute on something that stood out to me in Justice Thomas’s concurrence: the numbers. He pointed out that in 2024 the SEC obtained roughly 6.1 billion dollars in disgorgement orders, while returning about 345 million dollars to victims. He found it hard to see that practice as anything other than a fines regime.

But those figures aren’t directly comparable. An amount ordered by a court isn’t necessarily an amount collected from a defendant. An amount collected isn’t necessarily distributed in the same fiscal year. And money designated for distribution may not have been paid out yet.

At oral argument, the SEC said less than 6.1 billion dollars had ever been collected, though it couldn’t say how much, and suggested that 88 percent of what was collected was designated for distribution. But it also acknowledged that designation doesn’t mean the money was necessarily distributed in the end.

My point isn’t that Justice Thomas is necessarily wrong. It’s that the publicly reported figures alone can’t establish or disprove his criticism.

The 2025 figures show why. The SEC reported about 10.8 billion dollars in disgorgement and prejudgment interest ordered, including about 9 billion dollars in disgorgement from a single case, the Stanford Ponzi scheme litigation. After excluding the Stanford judgments and amounts deemed satisfied in other proceedings, the reported amount was about 1.4 billion dollars. The SEC reported returning about 262 million dollars to harmed investors that year. So most of the 2025 headline figure came from a single case. That’s why the categories matter: to evaluate the practice, we need to distinguish ordered, collected, designated, and actually distributed amounts.

Slide 8: Victims First Is Easiest to Defend

Target time: 1:16 (running total 10:14).

This is where my paper moves from the doctrinal problem to a practical answer. I don’t try to predict whether the Supreme Court will call disgorgement legal or equitable. Instead, I argue that the approach that appears easiest to defend under either outcome is to put victims first. If disgorgement is equitable, that approach follows Liu’s requirement that the award be for victims. If it’s legal, returning the money to investors is the clearest way to stay on the compensation side of the penalty line.

When distribution really can’t happen, the reasons should be documented. The SEC’s own Rule 1102(b), which governs administrative distribution plans, identifies practical considerations such as administrative costs and the number of potential claimants. It doesn’t by its terms answer what federal courts may do under Liu, and it governs administrative proceedings, but I suggest a court could look to those factors by analogy.

The conclusion of my paper has three parts. First, pay victims where payment is possible. Second, document why it isn’t possible when it isn’t, using the factors in Rule 1102(b). Third, report the amounts ordered, collected, designated, and distributed, so that others can test the gap Justice Thomas described. In the Eleventh Circuit, Spartan may make paying victims optional, but paying them remains the course that doesn’t depend on how the Supreme Court treats Spartan.

Slide 9: Return to the Opening Question

Target time: 0:55 (running total 11:09).

So I’ll end where I began. The question was, when the SEC makes someone give up money obtained through securities violations, where should that money go? After Kokesh, Liu, and Sripetch, we still don’t have a complete legal answer. We know a victim need not prove financial loss. We don’t know whether the new statutory remedy is ultimately legal or equitable, or whether equity permits Treasury deposits when investors can’t be paid. And we may learn more from Spartan.

The practical stakes of both questions meet at the same point: where the money goes. If disgorgement is equitable, returning money to victims is generally a duty. If it’s legal, that duty may disappear, but defendants could claim a right to a jury, and the penalty objections could sharpen wherever money doesn’t reach victims.

That’s why my conclusion is to pay victims where possible, explain why that can’t be done when it can’t, and report results that others can actually evaluate. Thank you.

Likely questions and answers

These answers use only what your paper says, and each one points to the Part of the paper it comes from. They’re written to be spoken, at roughly 20 to 40 seconds each. Where the paper doesn’t say, the answer says so, which is a fine thing to tell the room. Questions 1 to 5 are the ones I’d expect most.

1. Why does it matter whether disgorgement is legal or equitable? Isn’t that just a label?

It matters because the label decides which rules come with the remedy. If disgorgement is equitable, the rule from Liu that it be awarded for victims keeps binding. If it’s legal, that rule may fall away, but defendants could claim a Seventh Amendment right to a jury, and money that doesn’t reach victims could start to look like a penalty. So the label changes what the SEC can do with the money. (Parts I and IV.E-F.)

2. Who do you think is right, the Fifth Circuit in Hallam or the Second Circuit in Ahmed?

My paper doesn’t predict how the Court will rule, so I don’t want to claim more than it does. What I do is lay out both cases. The legal side is mostly textual: Congress put disgorgement in its own paragraph, with its own limitations periods, and if it were just equitable relief, the new paragraph would be superfluous. The equitable side is that Liu held the properly limited remedy was equitable, Congress acted six months later using the word Liu had applied, and gain-based relief has deep roots in equity. One more point from the paper: the equitable label may depend on how distribution works in practice. (Parts III.B-D.)

3. What did Sripetch actually decide, and what did it leave open?

It decided one thing: the SEC doesn’t have to prove that investors suffered pecuniary loss. Justice Gorsuch wrote that whatever else equity demands, it doesn’t require a showing of pecuniary loss before a court may award unjust profits. It left open both of my questions. The majority assumed without deciding that disgorgement under (d)(7) remains equitable, and on Treasury deposits it said the question remains for another day. (Parts II.C and III.A.)

4. The SEC says Liu’s limits don’t apply to (d)(7). Isn’t that inconsistent?

The paper says it arguably sits uneasily. The SEC conceded that disgorgement under (d)(7) is still limited to the defendant’s net profits causally connected to the violation. If that limit carries over, it’s fair to ask why the victims limit, which Liu announced in the same holding, doesn’t. At argument, Justice Gorsuch called the SEC’s position “pretty perilous,” but the majority didn’t resolve the dispute. (Part IV.D.)

5. What does “put victims first” actually require?

Three things. First, pay victims where payment is possible. Second, when it isn’t possible, document why, using the factors in Rule 1102(b). Third, report the amounts ordered, collected, designated and distributed, so that others can test the gap Justice Thomas described. It holds up under either outcome: Liu requires equitable awards to be for victims, and if the remedy is legal, money that reaches victims answers one of the reasons Kokesh gave for calling disgorgement a penalty. (Part V.C.)

6. What does Spartan hold?

The Eleventh Circuit held that the statute authorizes disgorgement paid to the Treasury, without requiring that the money benefit investors. Its reasoning was textual: (d)(5) says “for the benefit of investors,” (d)(7) doesn’t, and the court wouldn’t carry that limit into (d)(7). In the alternative, it held that equity permits Treasury-bound disgorgement where the parties stipulated that distribution was infeasible. That second holding is narrower, because it depends on a record of infeasibility. (Part IV.B.)

7. The Court asked the government to respond to the Spartan petition. Does that mean it will take the case?

No. A request for a response is a procedural step, not a grant of review. If the Court grants on the second question, the Eleventh Circuit’s holding on Treasury-bound disgorgement comes before it. If it grants only on the first question, which concerns Rule 10b-5, disgorgement is untouched. A denial leaves the Eleventh Circuit’s rule in place there and the question open elsewhere. The docket now shows the response due December 23, and my draft will be updated to say so. (Part IV.C.)

8. What counts as “infeasible”?

Liu left that open, including what showing of infeasibility would be required. The paper frames two views. On the strict view, an award paid to no investor can’t meet Liu’s standard. On the looser view, equity’s aim of stripping the wrongdoer of gains survives even when victims can’t be reached, so a court may send the money to the Treasury as a last resort. The paper doesn’t claim to know how often infeasibility actually arises, because the public sources don’t show it. (Parts IV.A and V.C.)

9. Does Rule 1102(b) apply to federal courts?

No. It governs administrative proceedings, and by its terms it doesn’t answer what Liu reserved, because Liu asked what equity permits and the rule states what the Commission has chosen to do. It’s still a public statement of the factors the Commission treats as making distribution unjustified, which are the cost of administering a plan relative to the available funds and the number of potential claimants. My suggestion is that a court deciding whether infeasibility has been shown could look to those factors by analogy. (Part V.B.)

10. Doesn’t $6.1 billion ordered against $345 million returned prove this is really a fines regime?

Justice Thomas read it that way, and I’m not saying he’s wrong. But the figures measure different things: money ordered, money collected, and money designated for or distributed to victims. The SEC told the Court that less than $6.1 billion was ever collected, though it couldn’t say how much, and it acknowledged that designation doesn’t mean the money was necessarily distributed. The public figures neither establish his criticism nor refute it. Only a shortfall, meaning money collected but not returned, would bear directly on it. (Part V.A.)

11. What’s going on with the 2025 numbers?

The SEC reported about $10.8 billion in disgorgement and prejudgment interest ordered for fiscal 2025, including about $9 billion in disgorgement in the Stanford Ponzi scheme litigation. After excluding the Stanford judgments and amounts deemed satisfied in other proceedings, the reported figure was about $1.4 billion. The SEC reported returning about $262 million that year. That’s why I say we need to separate ordered, collected, designated and distributed amounts before drawing conclusions. (Part V.A.)

12. Why does the Seventh Amendment come into this?

Jarkesy held that a defendant is entitled to a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties. Justice Thomas, relying on Jarkesy, would extend the right to disgorgement if it’s legal, because parties seeking “prototypical” legal relief get a jury. The SEC told the Court at argument that disgorgement is not a penalty and no Seventh Amendment right attaches. Justice Thomas answered that a remedy doesn’t have to be a penalty to be legal. The majority cited Jarkesy only when discussing penalties. (Parts III.C and IV.F.)

13. Does it help victims that Sripetch dropped the pecuniary-loss requirement?

Possibly. The paper says that by rejecting a pecuniary-loss requirement, Sripetch enlarged the class of victims, which may reduce the cases in which no one can be paid. But the paper also says the Court didn’t say that, so it’s an implication I’m flagging and not a holding. (Part IV.A.)

14. How does Kokesh, which called disgorgement a penalty, fit with Liu calling it equitable?

They answer different questions. Kokesh held that disgorgement, as the SEC then used it, was a penalty for limitations purposes, in part because it often didn’t compensate victims. Liu addressed that feature by requiring that an award be limited to net profits and awarded for victims. A remedy limited that way is compensatory in function, which arguably answers the penalty objection. But that holds only to the extent the money is actually returned to victims, which is why the equitable label may depend on distribution practice. (Parts II.A and III.D.)

15. Are these the SEC’s views?

No. The paper says the views are my own and don’t reflect the views of the Commission, the Commissioners or the staff, and it relies only on publicly available sources. The SEC’s public position, as the Sripetch majority described it, is that Liu’s limits don’t apply to (d)(7). I’m describing that position, not speaking for the agency.

16. What are the limits of your research?

It rests on public sources through November 16. I found no court of appeals decision since Sripetch that resolves whether (d)(7) disgorgement is legal or equitable. Spartan is the only appellate decision I located since Liu that squarely addresses Treasury deposits, and it predates Sripetch. The public sources I used don’t show how often infeasibility arises or what current practice is.
