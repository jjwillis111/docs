Theory of the case

Calvin looks like the buy-side source. He attended two Cilicon board meetings on the DigaView deal (Chronology of Cilicon-DigaView merger negotiations, 4b), bought Cilicon stock two days after the first (Quarterly statement from Valley Securities, 8b), and on June 3 moved $81,000 from his Valley account to account 326-015-8494 (8b). The Electronic Blue Sheet Report (3) lists that account as Andy Bernard's. It shows Bernard buying 3,000 DigaView shares on May 20, 27 and 28 for exactly $81,000 and selling them all on June 4 at 36.15. This is a working theory to test, not a conclusion.

Legal framing. Calvin works for the bidder, not the target, so the classical theory in the Instruction Memo fits poorly. Frame him under the misappropriation theory: he owed a duty of trust and confidence to Cilicon, the source of the information. The Formal Order (1) also cites Rule 14e-3, which reaches trading on nonpublic tender offer information that came from the offeror or its employees and does not turn on a breach of duty. Tipper liability usually also needs a personal benefit, so ask about his tie to Bernard and any money or favors. The next section sets out each theory in full. Confirm with the Enforcement attorneys which theory they want built.

What we must prove

The Instruction Memo (Preparation section) gives two theories of insider trading under the federal securities laws. Both are copied below as the memo states them. Rule 14e-3 follows, then a map from each element to Calvin.

Classical theory. Applied to corporate insiders (employees). We must prove that:

1.       the insider was in a position at his company that gave him/her access to information which he/she had a duty to use solely for a corporate purpose, and not to benefit himself or his acquaintances, before the information became public;

2.       the information was nonpublic;

3.       the information was material;

4.       the insider used the information to tip someone to trade company stock knowing that the information was not to be used for that purpose, or recklessly disregarding that fact.

Misappropriation theory. Applied to people who are not employees of the corporation. We must prove that:

1.       the tipper owed a duty of trust and confidence to the source of the information;

2.       the tipper breached this duty by sharing material, nonpublic information about the company with a tippee;

3.       the tippee traded in the company stock while in possession of, and using this information; and

4.       the tipper acted with at least recklessness.

Rule 14e-3. The Formal Order (1, Part III) cites it, but the memo does not cover it, so this is my summary and you should check it against the rule text. It applies once a person has taken substantial steps to commence a tender offer. We must prove that:

1.       the person possessed material information relating to the tender offer;

2.       the person knew or had reason to know the information was nonpublic and came, directly or indirectly, from the offeror, the target, or their officers, directors, employees or agents;

3.       the person traded in the target's securities, or passed the information on when it was reasonably foreseeable the recipient would trade.

No breach of duty is required.

How the elements map to Calvin. This uses the misappropriation theory, plus a personal benefit element that courts add for tippers and the memo does not list.

Examination outline

Each part below is one stretch of the 45 minutes, with its goal, the questions to ask and the document to show where there is one. The minutes beside each heading are a pacing guide; they total 43 and leave two for objections. Ask one open question at a time, listen, and follow up before moving on. Build foundation before substance. Documents are cited by Evidence List name and item number. Bold questions are the must-asks: if time runs short, protect those and drop the rest.

Objections and the Fifth Amendment

In the workshop, one Enforcement attorney plays Calvin's counsel and will raise these. The Instruction Memo's approach is the same each time: listen, decide whether the objection has merit, then either fix your question or tell the witness the question is still pending. The goal is a clean transcript, so imagine how each question reads typed up.

Foundation (lack of foundation, or assumes facts not in evidence)

What it means. Your question takes for granted a fact the record does not yet contain. Counsel objects because Calvin would have to accept your premise just to answer. The memo's example: asking "Did Ms. Jones ask Ms. Smith about the conflicting financial statements?" before anyone has established that conflicting statements exist.

What to do. Build a ladder. Before the question you care about, ask small, plain questions that put each needed fact on the record, one at a time. If counsel objects mid-exam, back up one rung ("Let me back up") and ask the missing fact first. The four places this is most likely to come up with Calvin:

The memo's rule of thumb: use common sense, and do not ask about topics out of the blue.

Asked and answered

What it means. Counsel says you already asked this and he already answered.

What to do. First decide honestly whether he gave a clear answer. If he did not, say so: "Mr. Calvin, I don't believe you answered my question. My question was..." You may also ask a slightly different question aimed at the same point. Do not ask the identical question over and over. If you believe your question was proper, ignore the objection and say: "Mr. Calvin, there is a question pending. Please answer it."

Example. You ask why he made the $81,000 transfer and he says it was a personal matter. Counsel objects. You say you do not believe that answered the question and ask what the purpose of the transfer was. If he stays vague, come at it from the facts around it: whose money it was, and what he expected back.

Compound question

What it means. Your question contains more than one question, usually joined by "and," so the answer is unclear.

What to do. Say "strike that," then ask the parts one at a time. Part 6 is the most exposed. A bad version: "Whose money was it, and why did you send it to Bernard, and what did he do with it?" A good version asks whose money it was, waits for the answer, then asks why he sent it.

Hearsay and form

These are not valid in administrative testimony, so you do not have to stop. Still, check whether the objection has merit. A "vague" objection to "What was that about?" is a signal to rephrase, for example "What did you and Bernard discuss the last time you spoke?" The memo says to modify your questions accordingly for a cleaner record.

Privilege (beyond the memo)

Counsel may instruct Calvin not to answer about conversations with Sorrell or with his own lawyers. Ask who was present, when it happened, and whether it was business or legal advice, then move on. Raise it with the Enforcement attorneys at feedback.

If he invokes the Fifth

Scott has said he will, and Calvin may too. Stay polite and do not argue the privilege. Ask each key question separately so every invocation is on the record. If it looks likely, move Part 6 up.

Staying in control

The memo says to act as if you control the proceedings and the record, without being too deferential to counsel, while staying polite and professional. Stay on the record. If you do go off it, summarize any substantive discussion when you return and have counsel agree to your summary.
